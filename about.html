The record was never
the interesting part.

He set the first one and noticed something nobody talks about afterwards: the certificate mattered far less than the six months before it.

A builder by profession, Manmohan Agrawal spent years watching what happens when a few thousand people are given one specific reason, one specific morning and one specific place to stand together. Schools that had never spoken picked up the phone. Wards that had lived beside each other for a decade discovered they could organise. Fifteen-year-olds took operational decisions that would not embarrass a project manager.

So the work changed shape. Instead of chasing records, he began building the gatherings that produce them — and treating the record itself as the receipt. Seven Guinness World Records later, across six independent certifying bodies, the method has not changed. Find the people. Do the paperwork properly. Make the day unforgettable. Leave something behind that outlasts the photograph.

Alongside the Carnival he serves as Secretary of the Rajasthan Affordable Housing Development Association and sits on the RERA Conciliation Forum — the same instinct, applied to rooms with fewer people in them.
